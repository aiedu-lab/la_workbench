# Parameterization

## Concept

### Degrees of Freedom

How many independent numbers does it take to pin down a point? A
point `(x, y)` in the plane, with no restrictions, has **2 degrees
of freedom** — pick `x` freely, pick `y` freely, and you can land
anywhere. Now require the point to sit on the curve `y = x^2`: pick
`x`, and `y` is no longer a free choice — it's whatever `x^2` says
it must be. Only **1 degree of freedom** remains.

$$
\text{degrees of freedom} = \text{the number of independent
choices you can still make}
$$

Every constraint you add — one equation relating the variables —
removes one degree of freedom. This is the idea underlying
everything below: a multivariable problem with `n` degrees of
freedom can often be walked through one degree of freedom at a
time, turning it into a sequence of ordinary, single-variable
problems.

### Parameterizing Curves, Lines, and Surfaces

**Parameterizing** an object means giving its remaining degree(s) of
freedom an explicit name — a variable `t` — so that every point on
the object is generated by plugging in a value of `t`.

A curve like `y = x^2` (1 degree of freedom) can be parameterized as

$$
x = t, \qquad y = t^2 \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad
\mathbf{r}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} t \\ t^2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

— sweep `t` across the real line and `r(t)` traces out the whole
parabola. A straight **line** through a point `p` in direction `u`
is parameterized the same way:

$$
\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{p} + t\,\mathbf{u}
$$

At `t = 0` you're standing at `p`; increasing `t` walks steadily in
direction `u`.

The same trick reduces a **surface** — a two-variable function
`f(x, y)`, 2 degrees of freedom — to a single-variable slice. Pick a
point `p` and a direction `u`, walk the line `r(t) = p + t·u`
through the domain, and evaluate `f` along the way:

$$
g(t) = f(\mathbf{p} + t\,\mathbf{u})
$$

`g(t)` is an ordinary single-variable function — the whole hill
`f(x, y)` collapsed down to whatever the hill looks like along one
particular path across it. Every tool from single-variable calculus
(derivatives, critical points) applies to `g(t)` directly, even
though `f` itself has two degrees of freedom.

### Constraint as Parameterization

A constraint is just a parameterization in disguise. Take
`f(x, y) = xy`, and constrain it to the curve `y = x^2` — exactly
the 1-degree-of-freedom curve from above. Substituting the
constraint in:

$$
h(x) = f(x, x^2) = x \cdot x^2 = x^3
$$

`h(x)` is a genuine single-variable function, and its derivative can
be found two ways: expand `h(x) = x^3` directly and differentiate
(`h'(x) = 3x^2`), or treat `y = x^2` as a path through `f(x, y)` and
apply the multivariable chain rule,

$$
\frac{dh}{dx} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} +
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dx}
$$

With `∂f/∂x = y`, `∂f/∂y = x`, `dy/dx = 2x`, this gives
`dh/dx = y + x(2x) = x^2 + 2x^2 = 3x^2` — the same answer, arrived at
by walking the constraint as a path rather than substituting it in
first. This is exactly the machinery in [Chain
Rule](partial_derivatives_multivariate_calculus.md#chain-rule) from
Partial Derivatives and Multivariate Calculus, now seen from the
other direction: a constraint *is* a parameterized path, and the
chain rule is what lets you differentiate along it without ever
fully substituting it in.

**Paper Problem (Degrees of Freedom):** For the line `y = 2x + 1`,
how many degrees of freedom does a point on it have? Write a
parameterization `r(t)` for it (letting `x = t`), and evaluate
`r(t)` at `t = -1, 0, 2`. In one sentence, explain why there's only
one degree of freedom even though the line lives in the 2D plane.

**Paper Problem (Walking a Surface):** For `f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2`,
starting point `p = (1, 0)`, and direction `u = (0, 1)`, write
`r(t) = p + t·u` and find `g(t) = f(r(t))`. Compute `g'(0)` by hand
and describe in words what it tells you about `f` at the point
`(1, 0)`, walking in the `u` direction.
